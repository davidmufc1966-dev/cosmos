Section I

The kinds of stars

Every world in this guide circles one. Stars come in a stunning range of sizes and temperatures — from failed sparks too small to ignite, to bloated giants larger than our entire inner solar system. Eight families, eight fates.

Brown dwarf

Failed star

Too small to ignite hydrogen fusion. They glow faintly from leftover heat — the bridge between giant planets and true stars.

ExamplesLuhman 16 · WISE 0855−0714 · Teide 1

Red dwarf

M-type

The smallest, coolest true stars — and by far the most common in the galaxy. Burn so slowly they will outlive every other star by trillions of years.

ExamplesProxima Centauri · Barnard's Star · TRAPPIST-1

Yellow dwarf

G-type · like the Sun

Steady, middle-aged main-sequence stars. Warm enough for liquid water, long-lived enough for biology to take hold. Our home star is one.

ExamplesThe Sun · Alpha Centauri A · Tau Ceti

Blue giant

O / B-type

Massive, scorchingly hot, and short-lived. Burn through their fuel in a few million years, then end as supernovae. The brightest stars in the night sky.

ExamplesRigel · Bellatrix · Spica

Red giant

Late-life Sun-like

What a star like our Sun becomes near the end. The core exhausts its hydrogen, the outer layers swell to a hundred times wider, and the surface cools to a deep orange-red.

ExamplesAldebaran · Arcturus · Pollux

Supergiant

Doomed colossus

The death throes of truly massive stars. Some grow so large they would swallow Jupiter's orbit if placed where the Sun is. All are destined to explode.

ExamplesBetelgeuse · Antares · VY Canis Majoris

White dwarf

Stellar ember

What's left after a red giant sheds its outer layers — an Earth-sized core, still glowing white-hot from residual heat, with no fusion left to power it. Cools for billions of years.

ExamplesSirius B · Procyon B · Van Maanen's Star

Neutron star

Supernova remnant

The crushed core left after a massive star explodes. A teaspoon of one would weigh a billion tonnes. Many spin hundreds of times per second, sweeping radio beams across the galaxy.

ExamplesPSR B1919+21 · the Crab Pulsar · the Vela Pulsar

The lives of stars

A star's fate is written at birth. The mass it forms with determines everything — how brightly it burns, how long it lives, and what it leaves behind. Three paths, three endings.

The Sun's path. In about five billion years it will exhaust the hydrogen in its core, swell into a red giant large enough to engulf Mercury and Venus, then puff away its outer layers as a glowing planetary nebula. What remains is a slowly cooling white dwarf about the size of Earth.

Massive stars live fast and end violently. Their cores grow heavy with iron until gravity overwhelms the outward pressure of fusion. The star collapses in less than a second, and the rebound detonates a supernova bright enough to briefly outshine an entire galaxy. The crushed core is left behind: a neutron star, or — if heavy enough — a black hole.

Section III

Stars that travel together

Despite the lonely impression our Sun gives, most stars in the galaxy are not solitary. They form pairs, triples, even sextuples — locked together by gravity, circling one another for billions of years. A small sampling of the most famous.

Alpha Centauri

Triple system

The closest stellar system to our own. Two Sun-like stars (A and B) orbit each other every 80 years, while a faint red dwarf — Proxima Centauri — circles them both from much farther out. Proxima hosts at least one Earth-sized planet in its habitable zone.

4.37 light-years · 3 stars · A: G2V · B: K1V · Proxima: M5.5V

Sirius A & B

Visual binary

The brightest star in our night sky travels with a hidden companion: a white dwarf the size of Earth but heavier than the Sun. Sirius A is twice as massive and twenty-five times brighter than our Sun; Sirius B is a stellar corpse, drained of fuel, slowly cooling forever.

8.6 light-years · 50-year orbit · A: A1V · B: DA2 white dwarf

Algol

Eclipsing binary

Known to medieval astronomers as the "demon star" because it visibly winks. Every 2.87 days, the dimmer orange companion passes in front of the brighter blue star, dropping the system's brightness for ten hours. You can see the dip with the naked eye if you know when to look.

93 light-years · 2.87-day orbit · A: B8V · B: K0IV subgiant

Cygnus X-1

X-ray binary

A blue supergiant in a death spiral with a black hole twenty-one times the Sun's mass. The black hole strips gas from its companion, and that gas spirals inward, heated to millions of degrees as it falls — emitting the X-rays that gave away the system in 1964. The first black hole ever identified.

7,200 light-years · 5.6-day orbit · Star: O9.7Iab · Companion: 21 M☉ black hole
